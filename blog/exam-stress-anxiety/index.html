How to Manage Exam Stress & Anxiety: 7 Evidence-Based Techniques

schedule 9 min read update Updated September 2026 verified By Prince Kumar

Quick answer

Exam stress is the worry and physical tension that builds before a test; a little sharpens you, but too much floods your working memory and blocks recall. To manage it, work on two fronts: lower the anxiety (box breathing, a brain dump, real sleep, reframing nerves as energy) and raise your certainty (active recall, past papers and a realistic plan so you have proof you are ready). Right before the exam, do a two-minute brain dump, breathe slowly, read the whole paper, and start with a question you can answer.

What is exam anxiety?

Exam anxiety — often called test anxiety — is the mix of worried thoughts, physical tension and racing heart that shows up before or during an exam and gets in the way of showing what you know. It is incredibly common: in one large survey almost every student reported at least one symptom of exam-related anxiety, so if you feel it, you are firmly in the majority.

It usually appears in three ways at once: physical (racing heart, shaky hands, churning stomach), mental (blanking out, "I'm going to fail" thoughts, trouble concentrating) and behavioural (procrastinating, avoiding revision, or cramming in a panic). A small dose of this is not the enemy — a bit of nervous energy actually sharpens attention. The goal is not zero stress; it is keeping the stress at a level that helps rather than hijacks you.

Think of stress as a curve, not a switch. Too little and you feel flat and unmotivated; too much and you freeze. The sweet spot in the middle — alert but calm — is where you do your best work. Most of the techniques below are about nudging you back toward that middle.

Why exam stress hurts your recall

When you feel threatened, your body releases stress hormones and shifts into "fight or flight." That is brilliant if you need to run from danger, but an exam hall is not a physical threat — and this response actively works against you in three ways:

  • It clogs your working memory. Anxious thoughts ("what if I fail?") take up the same limited mental space you need to hold and manipulate information, so there is less room left for the actual questions.
  • It narrows your attention. High arousal makes you fixate on the threat — a hard question, the ticking clock — instead of scanning calmly for what you can do.
  • It blocks retrieval. The information is still in there, but panic makes it harder to reach. This is why a well-prepared student can suddenly "blank" and feel like they know nothing.

The encouraging flip side: because much of the damage comes from the anxiety and not from a lack of knowledge, calming the anxiety often unlocks marks you had already earned through studying.

7 evidence-based techniques to manage exam stress

These draw on approaches that consistently show up in psychology research and student advice from bodies like the Mayo Clinic and university learning centres. None need special equipment, and they work best when you practise them in the days before the exam, not just on the day.

1. Prepare properly with active recall

The single biggest source of exam calm is genuine confidence that you are ready — and that comes from effective revision, not more hours. Test yourself instead of re-reading: use active recall and spaced repetition and work through past papers under timed conditions. Each successful retrieval is evidence to your anxious brain that you do know this, which shrinks the "what if I fail" story.

2. Do a brain dump

Spend a few minutes writing out everything swirling in your head — worries, half-remembered facts, "don't forget" reminders. Expressive writing before a test has been shown to reduce the mental "noise" of anxiety and free up working memory. Do it the night before and again in the last ten minutes before the paper.

3. Slow your breathing

Slow, deep breathing is the fastest way to switch off the panic response. Try box breathing: inhale for four counts, hold for four, exhale for four, hold for four, and repeat for two minutes. A long, slow exhale is the part that signals your nervous system to stand down.

4. Reframe the nerves as energy

That pounding heart before an exam is nearly identical to the feeling of excitement. Instead of "I'm so anxious," try telling yourself "I'm fired up and ready." Reappraising physical arousal as helpful — rather than dangerous — has been shown to improve performance, because you stop fighting the sensation and start using it.

5. Protect your sleep

Pulling an all-nighter feels productive but wrecks the exact systems you need: memory consolidation, attention and emotional regulation. A tired brain is a more anxious brain. Prioritise seven to nine hours the night before, and treat sleep as part of your revision, not a luxury you trade away.

6. Move your body

Even a brisk walk burns off stress hormones, lifts your mood and improves sleep. You do not need a workout — a 20-minute walk on a study break, or the day before an exam, is enough to take the edge off the tension. Progressive muscle relaxation (tensing then releasing each muscle group) works well right before bed.

7. Cut the caffeine and the comparisons

Too much coffee or energy drink turns useful alertness into jitteriness that mimics and feeds anxiety. Ease off in the run-up to exams. Just as important, stay off the group chat comparing revision — someone always claims to have "finished everything," and it only spikes your stress. Run your own race.

Notice the pattern: techniques 1 and 2 build certainty, while 3–7 calm the body. You need both. Breathing alone will not fix genuine under-preparation, and being prepared will not help if panic stops you reaching what you know.

A 5-step pre-exam calm-down routine

Have a plan for the last hour so you are not improvising while anxious. Here is a simple sequence that works whether it is a class test or a final:

  1. Do a brain dump. Ten minutes before, write down every worry and every fact you are scared of forgetting. Getting it on paper clears mental space for the exam itself.
  2. Breathe slowly. Two minutes of box breathing to bring your heart rate down and switch off the panic response before you walk in.
  3. Reframe the nerves. Remind yourself the racing heart is energy that will help you focus — not a warning that something is wrong.
  4. Read the whole paper first. When it starts, skim every question, then begin with one you can answer well. An early win builds momentum and quiets the panic.
  5. Reset if you spiral. If your mind goes blank, put the pen down, take three slow breaths and move to an easier question. The answer usually comes back once the panic passes.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Cramming all night. Trading sleep for a few more hours of notes makes you more anxious and less able to recall. Stop in good time.
  • Waiting to "feel ready" before starting. The calm feeling comes after you start and see progress, not before. Begin with one small block.
  • Only re-reading notes. It feels productive but builds false confidence that shatters in the exam. Test yourself instead.
  • Treating all nerves as bad. Fighting every flutter of adrenaline wastes energy. Aim to ride the useful nerves, not erase them.
  • Suffering in silence. If the anxiety is overwhelming, telling a friend, tutor or counsellor almost always helps more than hiding it.

When to get extra help

Everyday exam nerves respond well to the techniques above. But sometimes stress crosses into something that needs more support. Reach out to your school or college counsellor, a tutor or a doctor if exam anxiety regularly stops you sleeping, triggers panic attacks, makes you avoid exams altogether, or persists no matter how well you have prepared.

favorite

Clinically significant test anxiety is real and treatable — approaches like cognitive-behavioural therapy work well. Asking for help early is a sign of good self-management, not weakness. If you ever feel unable to cope, contact a trusted adult or a local mental-health helpline straight away.

Key takeaways

  • Some exam stress is normal and even helpful — the aim is to keep it in the useful middle, not remove it.
  • Anxiety hurts marks mainly by clogging working memory and blocking recall, so calming it can unlock knowledge you already have.
  • Work on two fronts: build certainty (active recall, past papers, a real plan) and calm the body (breathing, brain dump, sleep, movement).
  • Have a fixed pre-exam routine: brain dump, breathe, reframe, read the paper, and reset if you spiral.
  • If anxiety is overwhelming or persistent, seek support — it is treatable, and reaching out early helps.

Frequently asked questions

What is exam anxiety?
Exam anxiety, or test anxiety, is the excessive worry, physical tension and racing thoughts that show up before or during a test and get in the way of performing. A little nervous energy is normal and even helpful, but when the worry is so strong that it blocks recall, disrupts sleep or makes you avoid studying, it has tipped into anxiety worth managing.
How can I calm down right before an exam?
Do a two-minute brain dump of everything you are worried about, then use box breathing — in for four, hold for four, out for four, hold for four — for two minutes to slow your heart rate. Reframe the nerves as helpful energy rather than danger. When the paper starts, read it all first and begin with a question you can answer to build momentum.
Does exam stress affect your memory?
Yes. High anxiety floods working memory with worry, which narrows attention and makes it harder to retrieve things you actually know — a well-prepared student can perform like an unprepared one. Lowering the anxiety with breathing, a brain dump and good sleep frees up the mental space needed to recall your material.
How do I stop procrastinating out of exam anxiety?
Anxiety-driven procrastination shrinks when the task feels smaller and the outcome feels more certain. Break revision into tiny blocks, start with a single 25-minute focus session on the easiest topic, and use active recall so you get proof you are learning. Seeing steady progress is what quiets the worry, not waiting to feel ready. Our guide on how to stop procrastinating goes deeper.
When should I get help for test anxiety?
If exam anxiety regularly stops you sleeping, causes panic attacks, makes you avoid exams, or persists no matter how prepared you are, speak to your school or college counsellor or a doctor. Clinically significant test anxiety responds well to support such as cognitive-behavioural therapy, and reaching out early is a sign of good self-management, not weakness.
