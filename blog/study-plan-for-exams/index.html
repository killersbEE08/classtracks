How to Make a Study Plan for Exams (That Doesn't Fall Apart in Week 2)

schedule 10 min read update Updated October 2026 verified By Prince Kumar

Quick answer

A good exam study plan answers three questions: how many hours do I really have, where should they go, and what do I do in each one? Count your days and daily hours, rate every subject by difficulty and confidence, and give the hard, weak ones the most time. Then split your time into three phases — learn → practise → revise — and fill your sessions with past papers and self-testing instead of re-reading. Spacing your revision over days beats cramming. The free study planner below does the maths for you.

Why most study plans fail

If your last study plan lasted about four days, you're not alone. The usual culprits:

  • Equal time for every subject. Your strongest subject gets the same hours as the one you're failing — because it feels nicer to study.
  • Topics, not tasks. "Monday: Chemistry" doesn't tell you what to do when you sit down, so you re-read notes and call it studying.
  • Zero slack. One sick day and the whole plan is "ruined", so you abandon it.
  • Practice comes last. Past papers get saved for the final two days — exactly when you have no time left to fix what they reveal.

The plan below fixes all four: weighted hours, specific tasks, built-in rest days, and practice that starts early.

The science: what actually works

You don't need to guess which study methods are worth your hours. Researchers have tested them for decades:

  • Self-testing and spacing win. A landmark 2013 review in Psychological Science in the Public Interest (Dunlosky and colleagues) evaluated ten popular study techniques. Only two got the top "high utility" rating: practice testing (active recall) and distributed practice (spreading study over time). Re-reading and highlighting were rated low utility — the two things most students do most.
  • Spacing beats cramming. A 2006 meta-analysis by Cepeda and colleagues, pooling hundreds of experiments, found that spreading the same study time across separate sessions consistently produced better long-term recall than massing it into one session.
  • Mixing topics helps with problem-solving. Studies on interleaved practice (for example Rohrer & Taylor, 2007, on maths problems) found that mixing different problem types in one session led to better test scores than practising one type at a time — even though it felt harder.
info

What this means for your plan: start early enough to revisit each topic several times, fill your sessions with questions instead of re-reading, and mix subjects or problem types within a day. That's exactly how the 3-phase plan is designed.

      How to make a study plan in 6 steps

      1. Count your real study time. Days until the exam × hours per day you can honestly study. Subtract classes, work and one lighter day a week. That number — not a fantasy — is your budget.
      2. Audit every subject. List topics from the syllabus. Mark each one green (confident), amber (shaky) or red (no idea). Note which topics carry the most marks.
      3. Weight your hours. Red, high-mark topics come first and get the most time. Green topics get light review only. This one change does more than any study hack.
      4. Split into phases. Roughly 50% learning and filling gaps, 30% practice, 20% final revision. Short on time? Shrink the learning phase, never the practice.
      5. Turn topics into tasks. Not "Physics", but "Physics: 20 flashcards on optics + 10 past-paper MCQs". Every block should have a clear finish line.
      6. Schedule, then review weekly. Put blocks in your calendar with reminders. Every Sunday, re-rate your topics and move hours to whatever is still red.
      lightbulb

      Have several exams on different dates? Plan backwards from each one. In the last 3–4 days before an exam, that subject takes most of your time; between exams, keep the others ticking over with short active-recall sessions.

      The 3 phases of exam prep

      PhaseShare of timeWhat you do
      1 · Learn~50%Work through red and amber topics. Make short notes or flashcards as you go. Ask teachers or classmates about anything still unclear.
      2 · Practise~30%Past papers, practice questions, flashcards and explaining topics out loud. Mark your answers and add every mistake to a "weak spots" list.
      3 · Revise~20%One-page summaries, formula sheets, your weak-spots list and one timed paper. Lighter days, more sleep, no new topics.

      What to do in each study session

      A plan only works if each block is used well. A simple 50-minute session:

      1. 2 min — set the target. Write exactly what you'll finish ("12 questions from chapter 4").
      2. 5 min — warm-up recall. Without notes, write what you remember from last time. This is spaced repetition in action.
      3. 35 min — main task. Questions, past papers or learning a new topic — then close the book and explain it back.
      4. 8 min — check and log. Mark your work and add mistakes to your weak-spots list. That list decides tomorrow's plan.

      Then a 10-minute break away from your phone. To go deeper on the techniques, read active recall and spaced repetition, and keep your blocks on track with the Pomodoro timer.

      Fallen behind? How to recover

      Every plan slips. What matters is what you do next:

      • Don't add the missed hours on top. Doubling up tomorrow leads to burnout. Re-plan the remaining days instead — re-run the planner above with the new date.
      • Cut by marks, not by mood. Drop low-mark, green topics first. Keep high-mark red topics and past papers.
      • Use your rest day as a buffer — once. Then protect it again. Rest isn't wasted time; it's what keeps you going for another week.
      • Feeling overwhelmed? That's normal before exams. Our guide to managing exam stress has quick techniques that help, and beating burnout covers when to ease off.

      Key takeaways

      • Start with your real hour budget, then weight it toward hard subjects where you feel weak.
      • Split your time into learn (~50%), practise (~30%) and revise (~20%).
      • Practice testing and spacing are the best-supported study techniques; re-reading and highlighting are not.
      • Every block needs a specific task, and past papers should start early, not on the last day.
      • Behind schedule? Re-plan by marks, don't just stack extra hours.

      Frequently asked questions

      How do I make a study plan for exams?
      Count the days until your exam and the hours you can study each day. List your subjects, rate each by difficulty and confidence, and give more time to hard, weak subjects. Split the time into three phases — learn, practise with past papers and active recall, then final revision. Put specific blocks in your calendar and review the plan weekly.
      How early should I start studying for exams?
      For final or board exams, 4–6 weeks is ideal and 2–3 weeks is workable. For a single mid-term, 1–2 weeks is usually enough. Starting earlier lets you space your revision, which research shows improves long-term retention compared with cramming.
      How many hours a day should I study for exams?
      Around 4–6 focused hours a day is realistic for most students in exam season, in blocks of 25–50 minutes with breaks. Beyond that you usually hit diminishing returns, especially if it costs you sleep. See how many hours you should study.
      Can I make a study plan in 2 weeks?
      Yes. Spend about the first 4 days learning your weakest topics, the next 7 days on past papers and active recall across all subjects, and the last 3 days on summaries, weak spots and sleep. Prioritise high-mark topics and skip low-value re-reading. The 2-week template above lays it out day by day.
      What is the best study technique for exams?
      Practice testing (active recall) and distributed practice (spaced repetition) have the strongest evidence. A major 2013 review by Dunlosky and colleagues rated both "high utility", while re-reading and highlighting were rated "low utility".
