How to Balance a Part-Time Job and Studies (+ Student Work Limits by Country)

schedule 9 min read update Visa rules checked October 2026 verified By Prince Kumar

Quick answer

For most full-time students, 10–15 hours of paid work a week is manageable — and some research finds students working 10–19 hours do as well as non-working students. Problems usually start around 20 hours and get serious beyond 28. On a student visa, the law sets a hard cap: typically 20 h/week in the UK, Germany and Ireland, 20 h on campus in the US, 24 h off campus in Canada and 48 h a fortnight in Australia. Around the world, working while studying is normal — to pay rent in London, cover tuition in Toronto, send money home, or simply get experience on a CV. The question isn't whether to work, but how much, and how to protect your grades while you do. This guide works for any student, anywhere: domestic students juggling a weekend job, and international students who also need to stay inside their visa rules.

How many hours is too many? The research

A moderate job doesn't have to hurt your grades. A study of college students by Dundes and Marx (2006) found that students working 10 to 19 hours a week had higher grades on average than students who didn't work at all. The likely reason: a job forces you to plan and use time more efficiently.

Beyond about 20 hours, the costs grow. A 2023 study in Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis, following 4,418 US students, found that after accounting for background and prior achievement, harmful effects on outcomes began at about 20 hours a week and became more severe at 28 hours or more.

fact_check

What this means for you: 10–15 hours is a sensible starting point for a full-time student. Around 20 hours can work with a light course load and a well-planned week. More than that is a real risk to your grades — reduce it before exams, or consider whether your course load is realistic.

Hours aren't the only factor. Night shifts that cut into sleep, unpredictable rosters, and long commutes can hurt more than the raw number suggests. A 12-hour week of fixed, on-campus shifts is very different from 12 hours of last-minute late-night shifts across town.

Know your real study load first

Most students underestimate how many hours their course needs. Credit systems around the world assume a full-time course is roughly a full-time job:

SystemWhat one credit meansFull-time load ≈ per week
US credit hour (also used in many countries)About 1 hour in class + 2 hours of outside work per week15 credits ≈ 45 hours
European ECTS25–30 hours of total work30 ECTS per semester ≈ 35–45 hours
UK creditsAbout 10 notional hours of learning120 credits a year ≈ 40 hours in term

Real students often study less than these figures — but they're a useful ceiling. If your classes are 15 hours a week, plan for at least 15–30 more hours of reading, assignments and revision. Not sure how much you should be studying? See how many hours to study a day.

    Common traps: limits usually apply across all jobs combined; "week" may mean a fixed Monday–Sunday week; unpaid internships and volunteering can count as work; and self-employment or freelancing is often banned. This guide is general information, not immigration advice — ask your university's international office if in doubt.

    7 ways to balance a part-time job and studies

    1. Start with your study load, not your pay goal. Block classes and study first, then see how many work hours genuinely fit. Use the calculator above.
    2. Ask for fixed, predictable shifts. The same shifts every week let you build a routine. Tell employers your exam dates early — many will adjust.
    3. Prefer flexible or on-campus jobs. Library, lab, tutoring, campus café or remote research-assistant roles cut commute time and often understand exam seasons.
    4. Time-block study like a shift. Put study sessions in your calendar as fixed appointments. Our time blocking guide shows how.
    5. Use small gaps. Twenty minutes on the bus or before a shift is enough for flashcards or reviewing notes with active recall.
    6. Cut hours before exams. Plan two to four lighter weeks around finals. A few lost shifts cost less than a retake.
    7. Review every month. If grades slip, you're always tired or you're behind on readings, cut hours or change the job before the semester gets away from you.

    Key takeaways

    • 10–15 work hours a week is manageable for most full-time students; risks grow from about 20 hours and more so beyond 28.
    • A full-time course is roughly 35–45 hours of work a week in the US, ECTS and UK credit systems.
    • Student visa limits are legal caps: commonly 20 h/week (UK, Germany, Ireland, US on campus), 24 in Canada, 48 per fortnight in Australia.
    • Plan study first, ask for fixed shifts and cut hours before exams.
    • Review your balance monthly and adjust before your grades slip.

    Frequently asked questions

    How many hours a week can a student work without hurting grades?
    About 10–15 hours is manageable for most full-time students, and one study found students working 10–19 hours did better than non-workers. A 2023 US study found negative effects starting around 20 hours, worsening beyond 28.
    How many hours can international students work?
    It depends on the country: during term, 20 h/week in the UK, Germany and Ireland; 20 h on campus in the US; 24 h off campus in Canada; 48 h per fortnight in Australia; 25 in New Zealand; 28 in Japan; 16 in the Netherlands. Check the official page for your country.
    What happens if I work more than my visa allows?
    You break your visa conditions, which can lead to cancellation, refused extensions or problems with future applications. Track your hours across all jobs.
    How do I balance a part-time job and studies?
    Plan your study load first, cap job hours to fit, ask for fixed shifts, time-block study like appointments, cut hours before exams and review monthly.
    How many hours does a full-time course need?
    Roughly 35–45 hours a week including classes. A US credit assumes about 1 hour in class plus 2 outside per week; one ECTS credit is 25–30 hours; one UK credit is about 10 hours.
