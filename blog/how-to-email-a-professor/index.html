How to Email a Professor (6 Templates + Free Email Builder)

schedule 10 min read update Updated October 2026 verified By Prince Kumar

Quick answer

A good email to a professor is short, specific and polite. Use a subject line with the course code and purpose ("CS101 – Missed lecture on 4 Nov – Aisha Khan"). Open with "Dear Professor/Dr [Surname]". Say who you are and what you need in the first two lines, add one or two lines of context, suggest a next step, and sign with your full name and student ID. Send it from your university email and wait two working days before following up. Research shows tone matters: overly casual emails make instructors see students as less credible and less worth helping (Stephens et al., 2009). Use the free email builder or copy a template.

Emailing a professor feels high-stakes. You want an extension, a meeting or a reference letter, and you're writing to someone who gets dozens of student emails a day. The good news: professors aren't looking for perfect writing. They want to know quickly who you are, what you need and what you've already tried. This guide gives you a structure that works at any university, how to address academics in different countries, six copy-paste templates and a builder that writes the first draft for you.

Before you write: 3 checks

  1. Is the answer already in the syllabus? Deadlines, word counts, late-work rules and office hours are usually there. "As the syllabus says…" is a reply nobody wants to get.
  2. Is email the right channel? Some courses want questions on a forum (Moodle, Canvas, Teams), or sent to a teaching assistant first. Quick questions are often easier in office hours.
  3. Are you sending from your university account? It proves you're a student on the course and is less likely to land in spam. Many universities only reply to official student emails.

The 5-sentence rule: most emails to professors fit in five sentences or fewer. If yours is longer, it may be better as an office-hours conversation. Put the request in the first two lines, because many people read email on their phone.

How to address a professor in different countries

The safest rule anywhere: use the title on their university staff page or the syllabus. If they sign their reply with a first name, you can usually follow their lead next time. Norms differ by country:

Country or regionUsual greetingGood to know
US and CanadaDear Professor Smith"Professor" is normal for any university teacher. "Dr" is fine if they have a PhD.
UK, Ireland, Australia, New ZealandDear Dr Smith"Professor" is a senior rank, so most lecturers are "Dr". Use "Mr/Ms" only if they have no doctorate.
Germany, AustriaSehr geehrte Frau Prof. Dr. Müller / Dear Professor MüllerTitles matter more. Keep both titles in German emails.
India, Nepal, Pakistan, BangladeshDear Professor Sharma / Respected Sir or Ma'am"Respected Sir/Ma'am" is common, but using their name is clearer and more personal.
Nigeria, Ghana, KenyaDear Professor Adeyemi / Dear Dr MensahUse the full academic title. Formal openings are expected.
France, Spain, ItalyMadame la Professeure / Estimado profesor / Gentile ProfessoressaIn English, "Dear Professor + surname" works.

Never open with "Hey", "Hi there" or their first name alone unless they've asked you to. If you don't know their gender, use their full name: "Dear Professor Alex Kim".

Why tone matters: what the research says

How you write changes how professors see you, not just whether they reply.

  • Casual emails cost credibility. Keri Stephens, Marian Houser and Renee Cowan (2009) showed instructors student emails written formally or casually. Casual emails bothered instructors more than they bothered students. They lowered instructors' opinion of the student's credibility and made instructors less willing to meet the student. Two habits bothered instructors most: not signing the email and text-speak like "RU" instead of "are you". The instructor's age made little difference.
  • Politeness signals competence. San Bolkan and Jennifer Holmgren (2012) tested emails using different politeness strategies. Polite emails led instructors to see the student as more competent.
fact_check

What this means for you: formal doesn't mean long or flowery. A clear greeting, a polite request, "thank you" and your full name are enough to be on the right side of both studies.

    6 email templates you can copy

    Replace everything in [brackets]. Keep each email to the point: one email, one request.

    1. Missed class

    Subject: [Course code] – Missed class on [date] – [Your name]

    Dear Professor [Surname], My name is [Your name], and I'm in your [course code] class on [day and time]. I was unable to attend the class on [date] because [brief reason]. I've downloaded the slides and will watch the recording, and I'll ask a classmate for their notes. Please let me know if I missed any announcements or in-class work I should make up. Thank you for your understanding. Kind regards, [Full name] Student ID: [ID]

    Email before the class if you know you'll miss it. If your course has a minimum attendance rule, check where you stand with the bunk calculator and read does attendance affect grades to see what a missed class really costs.

    2. Deadline extension

    Subject: [Course code] – Extension request for [assignment] – [Your name]

    Dear Dr [Surname], I'm [Your name] from your [course code] course. I'm writing to ask whether I could have a short extension for [assignment], which is due on [date]. [One-sentence reason, e.g. I've been ill since Monday and have a medical note.] I've already completed [what you've done, e.g. the research and the first half of the draft]. Would it be possible to submit it by [proposed new date]? I'm happy to follow the official extension process if there is one. Thank you for considering this. Kind regards, [Full name] Student ID: [ID]

    Ask as early as you can. Requests sent days before the deadline, with a proposed new date and proof of progress, are much easier to say yes to. Many universities have a formal extension or mitigating-circumstances form. Your lecturer may point you there.

    3. Meeting or office hours

    Subject: [Course code] – Meeting request about [topic] – [Your name]

    Dear Professor [Surname], I'm [Your name], a student in your [course code] class. I'd like to ask for a short meeting to discuss [topic, e.g. my plan for the final project]. I can't make your office hours on [day] because of [another class / work]. Would any of these times work for you: [time 1], [time 2] or [time 3]? I'm also happy to meet online. Thank you, [Full name]

    4. Question about an assignment

    Subject: [Course code] – Question about [assignment] – [Your name]

    Dear Dr [Surname], I'm [Your name] from your [course code] [seminar/section]. I have a quick question about [assignment]: [your specific question]. I checked the brief and the course page, which say [what you found], but I wasn't sure whether [the part that's unclear]. Thank you for your help. Kind regards, [Full name]

    5. Feedback on a grade

    Subject: [Course code] – Feedback on [assignment] – [Your name]

    Dear Professor [Surname], I'm [Your name] from your [course code] class. I received [grade] on [assignment] and I'd like to understand how I can improve before the next assessment. I've read the written feedback. Could you tell me the one or two changes that would most improve my work? I'd be happy to come to office hours if that's easier. Thank you for your time. Kind regards, [Full name]

    Wait 24 hours after getting a grade before writing. Ask how to improve, not "why did I lose marks". If you think there's a marking error, your university will have a formal appeal or review process. Use that.

    6. Reference or recommendation letter

    Subject: Reference letter request – [Programme/job] – [Your name]

    Dear Dr [Surname], I took your [course code] course in [term and year], where I [one specific achievement, e.g. received an A on the research project on X]. I'm applying for [programme, scholarship or job], and I'd be very grateful if you could write a reference letter for me. The deadline is [date], and the letter would be submitted through [portal/email]. I've attached my CV, the job or programme description and a short summary of my work in your course. I completely understand if you're not able to. Thank you for considering this. Kind regards, [Full name]

    Give at least three to four weeks' notice, send everything they need in one email, and send a thank-you note afterwards. Looking for opportunities to apply for? See how to find student scholarships and internships.

    Follow-up if you get no reply

    Reply to your original email after two working days

    Dear Professor [Surname], I wanted to follow up on my email below about [topic]. I know this is a busy time, so I've kept it short: [repeat the request in one sentence]. Thank you, [Full name]

    7 mistakes that get emails ignored

    • No subject line, or just "Question" or "Hi". Professors sort email by subject.
    • No name or course. "This is about my essay" doesn't help someone who teaches 300 students.
    • Text-speak and no greeting. "hey can u send the slides" is the exact style that lowered credibility in the research.
    • Asking what's in the syllabus. Check first, and say that you checked.
    • "Did I miss anything important?" It suggests the class might not be important. Ask what you should catch up on instead.
    • Long stories. One sentence of reason is enough. Offer proof if it's needed.
    • Emailing at the last minute and expecting a reply in an hour. Plan around a two-working-day reply time. Put your deadlines in a planner so you ask early. See how to manage college assignments.

    Key takeaways

    • Subject line: course code + purpose + your name.
    • Greeting: "Dear" + the title on their staff page + surname. "Dr" is the default in the UK and Australia, "Professor" in the US.
    • Put who you are and what you need in the first two lines. Aim for five sentences or fewer.
    • Sign with your full name and student ID, and send from your university email.
    • Casual emails make instructors see you as less credible. Polite emails make you look more competent.
    • Wait two working days, then reply to your own email with a short follow-up.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do you start an email to a professor?
    With "Dear" plus the title and surname on their staff page, for example "Dear Professor Smith" or "Dear Dr Smith". Then say who you are: your full name, course and section.
    Should I call my lecturer Professor or Dr?
    Use the title on their staff page or syllabus. In the US and Canada "Professor" is normal. In the UK, Ireland, Australia and New Zealand most lecturers are "Dr", because "Professor" is a senior rank. In Germany use "Prof. Dr.". If unsure, "Professor" is the safer choice.
    What should the subject line be?
    Course code, purpose and your name, for example "ECON 201 – Extension request for Essay 2 – Rahul Mehta".
    How long should I wait before following up?
    At least two working days, longer during exams or holidays. Then reply to your original email with a one-line, polite follow-up.
    Does email tone really matter?
    Yes. Overly casual emails lowered instructors' opinion of students' credibility and made them less willing to meet (Stephens et al., 2009). Polite emails made students seem more competent (Bolkan & Holmgren, 2012).
    Can I use ChatGPT to write it?
    Use it to check tone and grammar, but write the facts yourself, keep it short and remove generic or invented details before sending.
