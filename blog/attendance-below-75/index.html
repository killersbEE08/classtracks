Attendance Below 75%? Here's How to Recover

schedule 7 min read update Updated August 2026 verified By Prince Kumar

Quick answer

If your attendance is below 75%, most colleges can bar you from exams, add a fine, or require a medical/condonation certificate. The fix is math, not panic: work out exactly how many classes you must attend in a row to climb back — use the bunk calculator — then attend every one. If it's not recoverable this term, talk to your department now.

What actually happens if you drop below 75%

It varies by institution, but the common consequences are:

  • Exam debarment — you may not be allowed to sit end-of-term exams.
  • Fines or detention in some colleges.
  • Condonation — you can sometimes be “condoned” with a valid medical certificate, often only down to a floor (e.g. 65%).
  • Repeating the course or year in severe cases.
warning

Your college's official record and rulebook are the source of truth. Check your exact policy and current percentage before assuming the worst — or the best.

Why colleges enforce 75%

The rule exists to ensure students engage with enough teaching to pass and to meet regulatory or accreditation requirements. It's rarely negotiable at the individual level, which is why staying ahead matters more than appealing after the fact.

How to recover, step by step

  1. Get your exact number. Use the attendance calculator — “about 70” isn't precise enough.
  2. Calculate the climb. The bunk calculator tells you how many classes in a row you must attend to reach 75%.
  3. Attend everything. No optional skips until you're clear of the line.
  4. Track each class so your percentage updates and you can see progress.
  5. Prioritise low subjects if attendance is counted per subject.
lightbulb

The math is unforgiving early and forgiving late: the more classes remain in the term, the easier it is to recover. Act the moment you notice a dip, not at the end.

If you can't recover in time

Sometimes there aren't enough classes left to reach 75%. If so:

  • Speak to your department immediately — options shrink as deadlines approach.
  • Gather documentation (medical certificates, proof of college-duty absences) for a condonation request.
  • Ask about make-up classes, extra labs or remedial sessions.

Preventing it next term

Track attendance from day one and keep a buffer above 75% so a bad week doesn't sink you. See how to improve your attendance percentage for the habits that keep you safe.

Key takeaways

  • Below 75% you risk exam debarment, fines or having to repeat — policies vary.
  • Recovery is math: calculate how many classes in a row you must attend, then attend them.
  • Acting early makes recovery far easier; the end of term is too late.
  • If it's not recoverable, talk to your department immediately with documentation.

Frequently asked questions

What happens if my attendance is below 75%?
Depending on your institution, you may be barred from exams, fined, required to submit a medical/condonation certificate, or in severe cases asked to repeat. Always check your college's exact policy, as rules and any condonation floor vary.
How can I increase my attendance percentage quickly?
Attend every remaining class — there are no shortcuts, since your percentage is attended divided by held. Use a bunk calculator to see exactly how many consecutive classes you need to attend to reach 75%, then don't miss any.
Can attendance be condoned?
Many colleges allow condonation down to a floor (often around 65%) with valid documentation such as a medical certificate. It's discretionary and deadline-sensitive, so approach your department as early as possible.
Do cancelled classes count against my attendance?
No — a class cancelled by the institution shouldn't be counted in the total held, so it doesn't drag your percentage down. Make sure your tracking excludes cancelled classes; ClassTrack does this automatically.
How much buffer above 75% should I keep?
Aim to stay a few points clear — around 80% or higher — so a single illness or emergency doesn't push you under. Tracking from day one keeps the buffer visible.
