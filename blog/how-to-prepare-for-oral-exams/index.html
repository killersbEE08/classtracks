Oral Exams Are Back: How to Prepare for a Viva or Oral Exam

schedule 10 min read update Updated October 2026 verified By Prince Kumar

Quick answer

Because AI can now write a polished essay in seconds, more colleges are checking understanding face to face — with oral exams, vivas and "defend your work" sessions. To prepare, stop re-reading and start explaining out loud. Find out the format, list the questions an examiner would ask, teach each topic aloud without notes, know your own submitted work inside out, run at least two mock vivas, and answer with a simple structure: answer → reason → example. If you go blank, say what you do know and reason from there — examiners reward clear thinking, not perfect recall.

Why oral exams are back in 2026

For years, most college assessment moved towards take-home essays, online quizzes and assignments. Then generative AI arrived — and those formats stopped being a reliable signal of what a student actually knows. According to Times Higher Education, as many as 95% of students now say they use AI in their studies, up from 66% in 2024. A 2026 Lumina Foundation–Gallup study found 57% of US college students use AI tools in coursework every week, and 20% use them daily.

Instructors noticed the gap. As one AP report put it, homework started coming back perfect, but when students were asked to explain it, many couldn't. The response at a growing number of universities is a very old idea: ask the student to talk.

  • At Cornell, a biomedical engineering professor now uses an "oral defense" where students explain their work directly to an instructor — "You won't be able to AI your way through an oral exam," he told AP.
  • At Penn, staff describe "a massive shift toward in-person assessments," both written and oral, and the teaching centre runs workshops on oral exams.
  • At NYU Stern, one professor ran a final exam where an AI voice agent questioned each student about their own group project — and grilled them on what they personally did.
  • In the UK, universities such as Bath are moving to a "two-lane" model from 2026–27: open assessments where AI is allowed, and closed, in-person, invigilated ones where it isn't.

In India and many other countries, the viva voce never went away — lab vivas, practical exams, internship and project vivas, and thesis defences are standard. What's changing is that oral checks are spreading to more courses, and examiners are increasingly using them to confirm that you really did — and understood — your written work.

lightbulb

The good news: oral exams reward students who genuinely understand the material. Fluent explaining is a trainable skill, and a week of the right practice makes a dramatic difference.

What examiners are really testing

Most students prepare for an oral exam as if it were a spoken written exam — memorising answers to recite. That misses the point. Examiners usually want to see three things:

  1. Understanding, not recall. Can you explain an idea in your own words, and then explain it again a different way when asked?
  2. Reasoning. When a question is unfamiliar, can you think out loud from principles you know towards a sensible answer?
  3. Ownership. For projects and written work: did you make these decisions, and can you justify them?

That's why follow-up questions matter so much. The first question is often easy; the second ("why?") and third ("what if...?") are where understanding shows. Your preparation should copy that pattern.

The 7-day viva prep plan

Seven days is ideal, but you can compress this into three if needed — just keep every step. Add your viva date to an exam countdown so the days don't sneak up on you.

Day 1 — Find out the exact format

Ask (or check the course page): How long is it? Who examines — one person or a panel? Is it about the whole syllabus or your own submission? Can you bring notes, slides or your report? Is there a short presentation first? Knowing this removes half the anxiety and tells you where to spend your time.

Day 1 — Build a question map

List the 10–20 core concepts in scope. For each one, write four questions: What is it? Why does it matter? How does it work? What if...? (e.g. "What if the sample size were smaller?"). This gives you a realistic bank of 40–80 questions — the same kinds an examiner will reach for.

Days 2–4 — Explain topics out loud, without notes

This is the core of oral-exam prep. Pick a concept, close your notes and explain it aloud as if teaching a first-year student — the Feynman technique. Where you stumble, hesitate or wave your hands, you've found a gap. Check your notes, patch it, and explain again. Speaking is a different skill from writing; you must practise it out loud, not in your head. Pair this with active recall and spaced repetition so each topic comes back several times across the days.

Day 5 — Re-own your submitted work

If the viva covers a project, report, practical file or code, re-read it as if you were the examiner looking for weak spots. You should be able to explain every number, graph, method choice and line of code you submitted. Write down: "Why did I choose this approach over the alternatives?", "What are the limitations?" and "What would I do differently next time?" — these three questions appear in almost every project viva.

Day 6 — Run mock vivas

Ask a friend, classmate or senior to quiz you for 10–15 minutes using your question map, with follow-ups. Better still, swap: being the examiner for someone else shows you what good answers sound like. If no one is free, use an AI assistant as a question generator (see the AI section) and answer out loud, recording yourself on your phone. Do at least two rounds.

Day 7 — The day before

Do a light review of your question map, rehearse your answer to the opening question (often "Tell us about your project" or "Explain the main idea of..."), lay out what you need to bring, and sleep properly. A tired brain retrieves words slowly — exactly what you can't afford when speaking live.

How to structure a great answer

A simple structure stops you rambling and makes you sound confident even when you're nervous. Use A-R-E:

  • Answer — give the direct answer in one sentence first.
  • Reason — explain why or how, in two or three sentences.
  • Example — make it concrete: a case, a number from your project, a quick sketch on paper.
info

Example. Q: "Why did you use a bar chart here?" — A: "Because I was comparing totals across separate categories. R: Bar charts make differences between discrete groups easy to see, while a line chart would suggest a trend over time that doesn't exist. E: You can see straight away that Group C is roughly double Group A."

A few more habits examiners love: pause before answering (two seconds feels long to you, not to them), use the correct technical terms, and stop when you've answered — long monologues invite tougher follow-ups and hide your main point.

What to say when you go blank

It happens to everyone. What separates a good result from a bad one is what you do in the next five seconds. Don't bluff — examiners spot it instantly and will probe further. Don't freeze in silence either. Use one of these honest lines to buy time and keep the conversation moving:

  • "Can I take a moment to think about that?"
  • "Could you rephrase the question, or give me a hint about the context?"
  • "I'm not sure about that exact detail, but here's what I do know..."
  • "I'd approach it like this..." — then reason step by step from principles.
  • "I don't know that one, but I'd find out by..." — showing you know how to find answers is still a skill.

Examiners usually give credit for visible, sensible reasoning even when the final answer is incomplete. An honest "I don't know, but..." is far better than a confident wrong answer.

How to calm viva nerves

Speaking under evaluation triggers a real stress response — racing heart, dry mouth, the feeling that words have vanished. A few things genuinely help:

  • Practise under mild pressure. Mock vivas with a timer and a real person make the actual exam feel familiar instead of new.
  • Slow your breathing. A few slow breaths with a longer exhale than inhale before you walk in calms your body quickly.
  • Reframe the feeling. Research by Alison Wood Brooks found that telling yourself "I'm excited" before a stressful performance improved results compared with trying to calm down. Your body can't tell nerves and excitement apart — you can choose the label.
  • Have your opening ready. Knowing exactly how you'll answer the first question gives you a confident start, and the rest flows more easily.
  • Bring water and a pen. Sketching a diagram or jotting key words while you think is normal and helpful.

If exam anxiety regularly overwhelms you, our guide to exam stress and anxiety covers longer-term strategies — and your college counselling service is there for exactly this.

Using AI to prepare (the right way)

AI is a big reason oral exams are back — but used well, it's also one of the best practice partners you can have. The rule: use AI to question you, not to answer for you. Check your course rules first, then try prompts like:

  • "Act as a strict university examiner for [subject]. Ask me one question at a time about [topic], wait for my answer, then ask a harder follow-up."
  • "Here are my project notes. List the 15 questions an examiner is most likely to ask in my viva, including tricky follow-ups."
  • "Here is my explanation of [concept]. What's missing, wrong or unclear? Don't rewrite it — just point out gaps."

Answer out loud, not by typing, so you practise the real skill. Tools like ChatGPT and Gemini offer voice modes that make this feel close to the real thing — especially useful if your exam is AI-run. For more ideas, see how to study with AI and our list of the best AI tools for students.

warning

Memorising answers an AI wrote for you is the fastest way to fail an oral exam. The first follow-up question ("why?") will expose it. Practise explaining, not reciting.

Key takeaways

  • Colleges are reviving oral exams because AI makes written take-home work a weaker test of understanding.
  • Examiners test understanding, reasoning and ownership — not memorised answers.
  • Prepare by explaining topics out loud without notes, not by re-reading.
  • Know your own submitted work well enough to justify every choice.
  • Answer with answer → reason → example, and reason out loud when you're unsure.
  • Use AI as an examiner that questions you, never as a script to memorise.

Frequently asked questions

How do I prepare for a viva in one day?
Focus on the highest-value material: the core definitions and principles, and your own submitted work if the viva is about a project or practical. Explain each key topic out loud without notes, list ten questions you'd ask if you were the examiner and answer them aloud, then stop early and sleep. Recalling material out loud is far more useful at this stage than re-reading.
What questions are asked in a viva?
Most viva questions fall into a few types: definitions (what is X?), explanations (how does X work?), justification (why did you choose this method?), application, comparison (how is X different from Y?) and extension (what would happen if...? what are the limitations?). For a project viva, expect questions about your own decisions, results and what you'd do differently.
What should I say if I don't know the answer?
Don't bluff and don't freeze. Say something honest that keeps the conversation moving — "I'm not sure about that exact point, but here's what I do know..." or "Can I think about that for a moment?" — then reason out loud from what you know. Examiners usually give credit for clear reasoning even when the final answer is incomplete.
Why are universities bringing back oral exams?
Because AI tools can produce polished essays and homework, written take-home work is a weaker signal of what a student actually understands. Oral exams and in-person assessments let instructors check understanding directly by asking students to explain and defend their work in real time.
Can I use AI to prepare for an oral exam?
Yes, if your course rules allow it and you use it to quiz yourself rather than to write answers for you. Ask an AI assistant to act as an examiner, generate likely questions from your notes and point out gaps in your explanations. The goal is to make you better at explaining, not to memorise answers it produces.
